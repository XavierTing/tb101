Transaction Banking 101

Transaction banking is the everyday banking a company uses to hold cash, pay suppliers, collect from customers, finance trade, and manage currencies.

What is Transaction Banking?

Transaction banking is the financial bloodstream of every business — moving cash in, out, and around, every day. And it's where banks earn some of their biggest, steadiest profits.

What it is

The bank's everyday toolkit for businesses

Think of a bank as a hardware store. Loans and credit cards are the big-ticket items. Transaction banking is all the everyday tools on the shelves.

It covers where the company keeps its cash, how it pays suppliers and staff, how it collects from customers, how it ships goods across borders, and how it juggles different currencies.

Problem it solves

Running a business is a logistics problem

By Friday, a mid-sized company has to pay 200 suppliers, collect from 5,000 customers, hold cash in 8 currencies, fund a shipment from Vietnam, and survive a swing in the SGD/USD rate.

No company wants to build that circulation system itself. The bank builds it once, then rents it to everyone — payment rails, accounts, financing, hedging tools.

How the bank earns

Four quiet but huge revenue streams

  • The deposit gap. Money sits in business current accounts earning the customer almost nothing — the bank lends that same money out at much higher rates. That gap (called Net Interest Margin, or NIM) is the single biggest moneymaker in transaction banking.
  • Transaction fees. A few cents per payment, a few dollars per wire, a small percentage per card swipe. Tiny each, huge in aggregate.
  • FX spread. Every time the business converts currencies, the bank quotes a slightly worse rate than its own wholesale cost. The small gap between the two rates is the bank's cut.
  • Trade financing interest. Short-term loans backed by real shipments and invoices — low risk, decent margin.

Banks love this business: low capital intensity, customers rarely switch, it keeps earning in a recession, and the deposits fund the rest of the bank.

Cash Management

The banking products a company uses every day to hold and move its money. Made up of four areas: accounts, payments, collections, and liquidity management.

Accounts

The bank accounts a business uses to hold its money — in SGD, in foreign currencies, or locked away for a fixed period to earn more interest.

What it is

Bank accounts, but built for businesses

Three flavours: an SGD operating account for daily flows, a multi-currency account for international businesses, and time deposits for surplus cash you can lock up at a higher rate.

Problem it solves

A safe, accessible home for working capital

Every business needs somewhere safe to keep its cash, plugged into the rails that send and receive money. International ones also need to hold multiple currencies without converting back and forth.

How the bank earns

The cheapest funding a bank can get

Money in current accounts earns the depositor almost nothing — the bank lends that same money out at much higher rates. The gap (Net Interest Margin) is the single biggest revenue line in transaction banking.

Multi-Currency Business Account

Customers who import auto parts from Japan, sell them in Europe, and pay their Singapore team in SGD — tired of losing 1% to FX on every conversion home.

What it isOne account that holds up to 13 currencies at once — like 13 wallets on one card.
Problem it solvesNo more opening a separate account in every country, and no more losing 1–2% to FX on every conversion.
How the bank earnsEach currency balance gets lent out, and the bank pockets the FX spread when customers eventually convert.

SGD Current Account

A founder incorporated her catering company yesterday and needs an account today to receive customer deposits and pay her chef on Monday.

What it isThe default business chequing account in SGD. Fees are usually waived above a minimum balance. Note: SDIC deposit insurance covers individuals and charities only — corporate accounts are not insured.
Problem it solvesWithout it, the business can't transact. Plugged into every Singapore payment rail (FAST, PayNow, GIRO, MEPS).
How the bank earnsMoney sitting here costs the bank almost nothing — and it gets lent out at full market rates.

Foreign Currency Call Account

A trader holds USD reserves they may need on a few hours' notice, but doesn't want the money sitting in a zero-interest current account.

What it isA single-currency on-call account in USD, EUR, AUD, JPY, GBP, or another major FCY. Earns interest, but you can withdraw any time — no lock-up like a time deposit.
Problem it solvesA middle ground between zero-yield current accounts and locked-up time deposits — useful for FCY-denominated working capital.
How the bank earnsSpread — the bank pays a modest interest rate and lends the same currency out at higher rates.

Time Deposit (SGD & FCY)

A profitable design agency has S$2M sitting idle for the next six months. The owner wants a bit of return without taking any market risk.

What it isLock your money up for a set period — one month, six months, etc. — and earn a higher, fixed rate than a current account. Available in SGD and ten foreign currencies.
Problem it solvesEarns yield on cash that's just sitting there waiting to be used.
How the bank earnsPure spread — the bank pays you a fixed rate, then lends or invests that money at a higher rate.

Payments

All the ways a business can send money out — instant local transfers, batch payroll, urgent high-value wires, cross-border payments, and corporate cards.

What it is

A menu of payment rails, each with a sweet spot

Instant local rails (FAST, PayNow), cheap batch rails (GIRO), high-value rails (MEPS), cross-border rails (SWIFT, International ACH), and card rails (purchasing cards).

Problem it solves

The right rail for the right job

Paying 500 employees on the 25th, one urgent supplier on a Friday night, and a S$50M property settlement are three different jobs. Pick the wrong rail and you'll either overpay in fees or miss a deadline.

How the bank earns

Fees on volume, spread on cross-currency

Most payment products earn a small fee per transaction. Cross-border payments add a meaningful FX margin (the bank's cut on the currency conversion) — usually the biggest line. Card payments earn interchange — a small percentage of each swipe paid by the merchant's bank to the card-issuing bank. And while the money is in transit, the bank gets to hold onto it — a little extra revenue from the brief float.

FAST

A construction firm's emergency rebar delivery arrives on a Friday evening. The supplier won't unload until he sees the money in his account.

What it isSingapore's instant SGD payment rail. 24/7, including weekends. Per-transaction limits are set by each bank (typically S$200,000+ for corporates; some now offer up to S$1M).
Problem it solvesPay anyone in Singapore in seconds — no waiting for next business day, no calling the bank.
How the bank earnsSmall fee per transaction. Volume game.

PayNow for Business

A retailer needs to refund an unhappy customer who only wants to share their mobile number — not their full bank details.

What it isA layer on top of FAST that lets you pay using simple identifiers instead of long account numbers — UEN for businesses, mobile or NRIC for individuals.
Problem it solvesRemoves the friction (and typos) of exchanging account numbers. Critical for refunds, walk-up payments, and customer onboarding.
How the bank earnsSame fee model as FAST — but PayNow's convenience drives much higher volume.

GIRO

Salaries for 800 employees, paid on the 25th of every month. Not urgent — but it absolutely must arrive.

What it isA batch payment system. Upload a file today; the bank processes overnight; money lands 2–3 business days later. Cheap and reliable.
Problem it solvesPerfect for high-volume, scheduled payments — payroll, dividends, supplier batches, tax instalments.
How the bank earnsTiny fee per payment, enormous volume. The 2-day settlement window holds the money with the bank a little longer.

MAS Electronic Payment System (MEPS)

A property developer needs to settle a S$50M land purchase by 5pm today. No second chances.

What it isSingapore's central-bank same-day rail for huge, time-critical transfers. No upper limit. Final and irrevocable.
Problem it solvesFAST stops at S$200,000 per transaction. For property settlements, large acquisitions, and any seven-figure wire, MEPS is the only option.
How the bank earnsA higher per-transaction fee (the value justifies it), plus interest on the huge sums sitting with the bank during the few hours between sending and settlement.

Network Transfer

A treasurer at a Singapore HQ needs to move USD to the company's Hong Kong subsidiary — both held at the same bank.

What it isA flat-fee transfer between the bank's own branches and subsidiaries across the region (Hong Kong, Malaysia, Indonesia, China, etc.), settling the same day.
Problem it solvesSkips the global wire system — which charges $20–40 to each “middleman” bank that relays the payment along, and takes 1–2 days — for transfers between branches of the same group.
How the bank earnsA flat fee per transfer, plus deeper relationship lock-in across multiple markets.

Outward Telegraphic Transfer

A trading company pays €120,000 to a German machinery supplier for a CNC mill.

What it isA regular international wire over the global SWIFT network. 60+ currencies, tracked end-to-end with SWIFT gpi.
Problem it solvesThe default rail when no cheaper local option exists for the destination country.
How the bank earnsA flat fee, plus FX spread on the conversion — usually where the real money is.

International ACH (IACH)

A payroll firm pays USD salaries to remote staff based in the United States.

What it isA cheaper alternative to SWIFT for small cross-border payments. Drops your money into the destination country's local rail (ACH in the US, BACS in the UK, etc.).
Problem it solvesSlashes the cost of low-value international payments — great for global payroll and small supplier payments.
How the bank earnsFlat fee plus a smaller but reliable FX spread on the converted leg.

Virtual Purchasing Card

A finance manager needs to give the marketing team a one-time card with a S$5,000 cap for next month's industry conference.

What it isA digital, single-use or limited-use card. Set the spending limit, merchant category, and expiry up front. No physical plastic.
Problem it solvesEvery card is tied to a specific purpose with a built-in limit — no more loose physical corporate cards.
How the bank earnsInterchange on every swipe, per-card programme fees, and interest on the balance between spend and settlement.

Business Debit Card

A small services firm wants to give its operations manager a physical card for petty cash — office supplies, taxi fares, client lunches — with the spend pulled straight from the business current account.

What it isA Visa/Mastercard debit card linked directly to the SGD Current Account. Tap, swipe, or use online — no credit line, no monthly card statement to settle.
Problem it solvesLets staff make everyday business purchases without involving petty cash or a corporate credit card — with built-in spend caps and per-card controls.
How the bank earnsInterchange on every transaction. No credit risk, since the money comes straight from the company's own account.

Collections

All the ways a business can receive money in — and the tools that automatically match each incoming payment to the right customer and invoice.

What it is

Acceptance rails plus reconciliation tools

QR codes for walk-in customers, card terminals at the till, direct-debit mandates for subscriptions, virtual account numbers tied to specific payers, and APIs that ping the company's systems the second a payment arrives.

Problem it solves

Two problems at once

First: accept whatever payment method the customer wants to use. Second: automatically match the incoming money to the right invoice, so the finance team isn't reconciling 5,000 transactions by hand at month-end.

How the bank earns

A slice of every transaction, plus tools

Card and QR acceptance earn a merchant discount rate — a small percentage of every transaction. Reconciliation tools earn monthly subscriptions. And every collection product brings cash in faster, so it sits with the bank longer.

eGIRO

An insurer wants new policyholders to authorise monthly auto-debit during online sign-up — not three weeks later via paper form.

What it isA fully digital GIRO direct-debit authorisation. Customers approve in minutes, not weeks.
Problem it solvesRemoves the paper-form drop-off that costs subscription businesses customers — insurance, telco, gym, SaaS, anything recurring.
How the bank earnsSetup & maintenance fees, plus a per-debit fee on every collection.

Virtual Account

A property manager collects rent from 5,000 tenants and used to lose hours manually matching payments to the right unit.

What it isOne real account with thousands of unique account numbers underneath — each one assigned to a specific customer.
Problem it solvesWhen money arrives, the account number itself tells you who paid. No more manual matching.
How the bank earnsMonthly fees per virtual account, plus a deeper deposit relationship.

OneCollect

A hawker stall wants to accept PayNow from locals and Alipay from Chinese tourists — from a single QR code on a sign.

What it isA merchant phone app that accepts QR payments from PayNow, Alipay+, WeChat Pay, UnionPay, and ShopeePay.
Problem it solvesOne QR sticker on the sign instead of five.
How the bank earnsA small slice of each transaction (the merchant discount rate), varying by wallet.

Point-of-Sale Terminal

A restaurant wants a single device at the till that accepts any card, any wallet, any way the customer wants to pay.

What it isThe countertop or handheld card terminal — tap, swipe, or insert any major card or contactless wallet (Visa, Mastercard, PayNow, Alipay+, WeChat).
Problem it solvesOne device handles every payment type — no separate hardware per scheme.
How the bank earnsA percentage of every transaction, plus monthly terminal rental.

Business Deposit Card

A retail manager needs to deposit S$8,000 cash at 11pm without holding it overnight in the store.

What it isA card that lets staff deposit cash at any Cash Deposit Machine (CDM), 24/7, straight into the company account.
Problem it solvesNo risky overnight cash on premises; no branch visits during business hours.
How the bank earnsA small per-deposit service fee, plus the cash lands in the bank's books faster than waiting for an armoured-truck pickup to count and settle it.

Credit Notification APIs

An e-commerce platform wants to release a digital product the moment a customer's bank transfer lands — not 30 minutes later when someone checks the statement.

What it isA real-time webhook that fires the moment a payment arrives, pushing the alert straight into the company's own systems. Part of the bank's wider API catalogue (payments, FX, account info, sanctions screening).
Problem it solvesRemoves the delay between getting paid and acting on it — instant order release, instant service activation.
How the bank earnsAPI subscription fees, plus an integration so deep the company can't easily switch banks.

Liquidity Management

Tools that move cash automatically across a company's many accounts and subsidiaries, so idle balances and overdraft costs are kept to a minimum.

What it is

Tools that auto-move (or virtually combine) cash across accounts

Rule-based products that move cash — or just treat it as if it's been moved — across accounts, entities, and countries, so the group runs with minimum surplus and minimum overdraft.

Problem it solves

Fragmented cash costs real money

Big companies have dozens of accounts across many countries. Without these tools, one subsidiary sits on $20M idle while another pays 6% overdraft interest. Treasury could fix it manually — but only at high effort, never in real time.

How the bank earns

Platform fees and much deeper deposits

Setup and monthly platform fees are direct revenue. The bigger prize: balances get concentrated with one bank, making it the obvious choice for every other product too.

Cash Sweep

A regional treasurer wants every subsidiary's leftover cash to flow into the parent's central account by end-of-day, automatically, with no manual intervention.

What it isProgrammed rules that move cash above (or below) a threshold to (or from) a header account — intraday or end-of-day, on a schedule.
Problem it solvesNo more manual treasury work; no entity is overdrawing while another sits on idle cash.
How the bank earnsSetup and monthly platform fees. Bigger prize: deposits pile up at the parent account, giving the bank a much larger pool of cheap funding to lend out.

Interest Optimisation

A holding company doesn't want to legally pool subsidiary cash (taxes, audits, regulators get involved) — but still wants the yield benefit of looking like one big depositor.

What it isThe bank pretends, for interest-calculation purposes only, that all the entities' accounts are one big balance — and pays a better rate accordingly.
Problem it solvesCaptures the yield bump from pooling without any of the legal, tax, or accounting mess of actually moving money.
How the bank earnsThe bank still keeps a spread on the optimised rate; very sticky multi-entity relationship.

Notional Pooling

Subsidiary A has +$5M. Subsidiary B has −$3M. The CFO wants overdraft interest charged on the net $0 position, not on B's full $3M overdraft.

What it isThe bank calculates interest on the netted balance across entities — rather than charging full interest on each entity's gross position. No actual money moves.
Problem it solvesSlashes group-wide interest expense without triggering inter-company loans or breaching legal-entity boundaries. Note: notional pooling is not permitted in the US and is restricted in several other jurisdictions (e.g., China, India) — structures are typically booked in pooling-friendly hubs like Singapore, Hong Kong, the Netherlands, or the UK.
How the bank earnsSetup and monthly platform fees, plus extreme stickiness — the bank becomes the group's primary banker.

Trade & Working Capital

The banking products that help a company pay for goods, ship them across borders, and bridge the cash gap between buying stock and getting paid for it. Made up of two areas: trade finance and supply chain finance.

Trade Finance

Banking products that let a buyer and seller in different countries safely complete a trade. The bank guarantees payment in exchange for shipping documents, and lends short-term cash to bridge the gap between paying suppliers and getting paid by customers.

What it is

The bank standing in the middle of international trade

A toolkit of products with formal names — Letters of Credit (the bank promises the seller it will pay once shipping documents arrive), documentary collections (the bank releases documents only after the buyer pays or commits to pay), trust receipts (the bank lets the buyer take goods on credit while the bank still owns them), invoice financing (the bank advances cash against unpaid invoices), and bank guarantees. The bank acts as the trusted middleman — and lends short-term cash to bridge the gap between paying suppliers and getting paid by customers.

Problem it solves

The trust gap, and the timing gap

A Vietnam seller doesn't trust a Singapore buyer enough to ship US$2M of timber on credit. The buyer doesn't want to pay before seeing the goods. Trade finance bridges that trust gap — and the timing gap, since the buyer pays the supplier on shipment but doesn't collect from its own customer for 90 days.

How the bank earns

Multiple fees per deal, plus interest

Issuance fees on the document, commission on guarantees, handling fees per step, plus interest on any short-term loan. A single shipment can earn four or five separate fees on top of a financing margin.

Trade Finance Suite

A Singapore furniture importer buys US$500K of timber from an Indonesian sawmill. The sawmill won't ship without a payment guarantee. The importer won't pay until the timber is on a boat.

What it isA toolbox of products — Letters of Credit, documentary collections, trust receipts, invoice financing — that lets the bank stand in the middle of the deal and handle both the trust problem and the cash-timing problem.
Problem it solvesThe Letter of Credit gives the sawmill confidence that the bank (not the buyer) will pay, as long as the shipping documents match the agreed terms. Trust receipts let the importer take possession of the timber on credit, sell it to retailers, then repay the bank from the proceeds. Both follow a global rulebook (called UCP 600) that every major bank recognises.
How the bank earnsIssuance fee on the LC, document handling fees, plus interest on any short-term financing — multiple revenue lines per deal.

Invoice Financing

An electronics distributor has S$2M in unpaid customer invoices on 60-day terms — but needs cash this week to settle suppliers and keep the warehouse stocked.

What it isA short-term loan secured against unpaid invoices. Comes in two flavours: Sales (cash advance against invoices you've issued to your customers) and Purchase (cash advance against invoices you owe your suppliers).
Problem it solvesTurns receivables and payables into working capital — the company doesn't have to wait the full payment cycle for cash to flow back in.
How the bank earnsInterest on the advance, plus an arrangement fee. Risk is bounded by the underlying invoice.

Banker's Guarantee / Standby LC

A contractor wants to bid on a US$20M government tender. The tender rules require a 5% bid bond — but the contractor doesn't want US$1M of its cash tied up for months.

What it isA written promise from the bank: “If our customer fails to deliver, we'll pay you instead.” Performance bonds, bid bonds, and advance-payment guarantees are all variants.
Problem it solvesLets the company prove its financial strength to a counterparty without locking up cash collateral on every promise.
How the bank earnsA commission of 1–2% per year on the guarantee amount — plus a signed promise from the customer to repay the bank in full if the guarantee ever gets paid out. The bank puts up no cash unless the guarantee is actually called.

Supply Chain Finance

A programme where the bank pays a company's suppliers early at a small discount, then collects the full invoice amount from the buyer on the original due date.

What it is

A program that pays suppliers early at a small discount

The bank pays an approved supplier early at a small discount, then collects the full invoice from the buyer on the original due date. Variants finance either the seller's receivables or the buyer's payables.

Problem it solves

Misaligned working capital across the chain

Suppliers want cash now. Buyers want to delay. SCF gives both: suppliers get paid on day 10 instead of day 90, and the buyer keeps its 90-day terms.

How the bank earns

A discount margin, priced to the buyer's credit

The bank earns a discount margin on every advanced invoice — the small slice it shaves off the invoice value when it pays the supplier early. Because the ultimate payer is usually a big, reliably creditworthy buyer (the kind credit-rating agencies call “investment grade”), the actual risk is low — but the bank can still price the deal as if it were lending to the smaller supplier. Favourable economics.

Receivables & Supplier Finance

A multinational tells 200 suppliers: “Wait 90 days for our payment, or get paid on day 10 by our bank for a small discount.” Suppliers love it.

What it isA portal where suppliers see the buyer's approved invoices and can click to take early payment from the bank at a small discount.
Problem it solvesSuppliers get cash early; the buyer keeps its long payment terms; the chain relationship improves on both sides.
How the bank earnsA small discount taken from each invoice it pays early — priced as if it were lending to the smaller supplier, even though the real risk is on the big, creditworthy buyer.

Accounts Receivable Purchase

A Singapore exporter ships S$5M of components to a creditworthy MNC on 180-day terms but wants the cash off its books today — not just an advance against them.

What it isThe bank buys the receivables outright (a “true sale”) for up to 360 days. Different from invoice financing — the bank takes on the buyer's payment risk and the invoices come off the seller's balance sheet.
Problem it solvesCleans up the seller's balance sheet, improves working-capital ratios, and removes credit risk on the buyer.
How the bank earnsA discount on the purchase price (the bank pays less than face value), priced on the buyer's credit quality.

Foreign Exchange (FX)

Tools to convert currencies and lock in exchange rates ahead of time, so a company isn't exposed to sudden swings in the FX market.

What it is

Pricing, converting, and locking in exchange rates

Three building blocks: spot trades that convert currency at today's rate, forwards and swaps that lock in tomorrow's rate today, and options that act like insurance — pay a small premium now, only use the protection if the market moves against you. Together they let companies convert currencies on demand and take the risk of nasty surprises off the table.

Problem it solves

Currency risk is existential for international businesses

A company earning in USD and spending in SGD lives or dies by the exchange rate. A 5% adverse swing on a US$50M cost base is US$2.5M off the bottom line. Hedging takes that risk off the table — you fix tomorrow's rate today.

How the bank earns

The spread, on every single trade

Every conversion earns a spread — the gap between the bank's wholesale rate and the rate quoted to the customer. Forwards, swaps, and options each add their own hedging margin.

FX Contract Booking

A treasurer needs to convert US$500,000 to SGD this morning — she wants the live rate now, not a phone call back from a dealer.

What it isA self-service FX dealing screen with live, streaming rates. Click to convert.
Problem it solvesNo phone tag with the dealing desk for small and mid-sized conversions. Transparent, repeatable pricing.
How the bank earnsA spread on every booked trade.

Channels & Connectivity

The web portal, mobile app, file-transfer pipes, and APIs that connect a company's people and systems to the bank.

What it is

Web portals, mobile apps, file transfers, and APIs

The web portal treasury logs into, the mobile app senior staff approve from, the automatic file-transfer pipes from accounting software, and the APIs that let the company's systems talk directly to the bank.

Problem it solves

Friction is the silent killer of banking usage

Nobody wants to manually re-type 5,000 payments. Nobody wants to wait for the boss to be back at her desk to release a wire. The smoother the channel, the more transactions happen — and the more wired-in the bank becomes.

How the bank earns

Mostly indirect — but the most powerful lever

Direct channel fees are modest. The real value: channels drive transaction volume across every other product, and deep integrations (especially file transfers and APIs) make the bank operationally painful to replace.

Enterprise Web Portal

A regional treasurer at a holding company logs in once each morning to oversee cash, approvals, and FX across 15 subsidiaries in six countries.

What it isThe flagship web portal for big corporates — payments, collections, trade documents, FX, liquidity, and reporting all in one place.
Problem it solvesOne log-in instead of fifteen. Built-in approval workflows that match the company's own controls — e.g. one person prepares a payment, a second person reviews and approves it (a “maker/checker” flow), with limits on how much each role can authorise.
How the bank earnsIndirect — drives volume across every other product. Very high stickiness once a treasury runs on it.

Business App

A managing director is on a flight to Tokyo and needs to approve a US$2M payment from her phone before takeoff.

What it isA mobile-optimised companion to the portal, focused on viewing balances and approving transactions on the go.
Problem it solvesRemoves the bottleneck of senior approvers being away from their desks.
How the bank earnsIndirect — but every minute of friction removed means another transaction completed across the bank's fee-earning products.

Host-to-Host (H2H)

A payroll bureau processes 50,000 salaries each month and isn't about to type them into a portal one by one.

What it isA secure automated file-transfer pipe between the company's accounting system and the bank, using standard formats like ISO 20022 and MT940.
Problem it solvesRemoves manual file handling for high-volume payments and statements. Straight-through processing, no humans in between.
How the bank earnsSetup and monthly connection fees, plus per-transaction revenue on the enormous volumes that flow over the pipe.

Banking APIs (Developer Portal)

An e-commerce platform wants to issue refunds and pull live account balance from inside its own admin panel — no banking portal in sight.

What it isA REST API catalogue — developers can initiate payments, check balances, look up transaction status, and receive payment alerts directly from their own code.
Problem it solvesEmbeds banking deep into the company's tech stack — no portal logins, no file uploads, no humans in the loop.
How the bank earnsAPI usage fees plus the deepest stickiness possible — once developers build against your APIs, the company can't easily switch.
